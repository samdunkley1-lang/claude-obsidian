## Key Question 1: Buyer map — who sponsors, evaluates, signs and vetoes at a USD 5-100bn asset manager and at a hedge fund

### Takeaway
No survey-grade source on "who signs for software at asset managers" was retrievable this session; what the evidence does establish is that in 2026 the Chief Compliance Officer is a live, motivated budget-holder for anything framed as AI governance (85% of 411 SEC-registered adviser compliance teams name AI their top 2026 priority), that Deloitte's 2026 outlook expects AI purchases to be vetted by a central, enterprise-wide AI team demanding audit trails, data lineage and human-in-the-loop thresholds, and that InfoSec/legal reviews are the stage that dominates the second half of every financial-services deal.

### Cited Findings
- AI was identified by 85% of respondents as the top compliance topic for 2026, a 28-percentage-point rise on 2025 and the most dominant single response in the survey's 21-year history; 72% of firms increased compliance testing around AI, 80% have formally adopted AI tools, 86% have acceptable-use policies (up from ~64% in fall 2025) and 86% keep an inventory of AI tools; the survey (IAA, ACA Group, Yuter Compliance Consulting) ran late April-May 2026 across compliance professionals at 411 investment adviser firms — [ACA Group press release](https://www.acaglobal.com/news-and-announcements/survey-ai-dominates-compliance-priorities-at-historic-margin-as-firms-move-from-awareness-to-action/); also reported by [Law360](https://www.law360.com/assetmanagement/articles/2506796) and [NAPA](https://www.napa-net.org/news/2026/7/ai-emerges-as-top-compliance-priority-among-ria-firms/)
- Deloitte's 2026 investment management outlook says leading organisations "could turn to general, generative, and agentic AI managed by a central team with enterprisewide authority", and that such central teams should prioritise governance artifacts: a model inventory, model risk assessments, clear data lineage, rigorous vendor vetting, defined human-in-the-loop thresholds, audit trails and incident-response protocols — [Deloitte 2026 investment management outlook](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/financial-services/financial-services-industry-outlooks/investment-management-industry-outlook.html)
- The SEC's 2024 amendments to Regulation S-P require investment advisers to oversee service providers with access to customer information; advisers with USD 1.5bn+ AUM had to comply by 3 December 2025, smaller advisers by 3 June 2026 — [Zocks blog on SOC 2 for advisers](https://www.zocks.io/blog/soc-2-compliance-for-financial-advisors-what-you-need-to-know)
- Enterprise deals in financial services move slowly because "more stakeholders must agree, legal and security reviews add weeks, and each additional approver is another point where the deal can stall"; buyers "arrive with security questionnaires running 200-400 questions and may ask for SOC 2 Type II, penetration test results, and data residency documentation before procurement will even schedule a call" — search-tool synthesis of [Stackmatix B2B sales-cycle benchmarks](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/b2b-sales-cycle-benchmarks), [FinTechtris GTM playbook for selling B2B fintech to FIs](https://www.fintechtris.com/blog/b2b-fintech-sales-enterprise-financial-institutions) and [Andrew Turner, "The 9+ Month Reality of Enterprise Sales"](https://andrewjturner.substack.com/p/the-9-month-reality-of-enterprise) (single-page attribution approximate; pages could not be fetched)
- Generic role definitions only: the CIO (technology) manages IT infrastructure; the COO oversees daily operations and governing policies; the CCO oversees compliance responsibilities and employee adherence to internal policies — [TechTarget C-level definition](https://www.techtarget.com/searchcio/definition/C-level), [PeopleForce job-abbreviation guide](https://peopleforce.io/blog/decoding-ceo-cfo-coo-cmo-and-more-understanding-common-job-abbreviations); an investment CIO is a board-level executive responsible for the portfolio, growth strategy and "recognizing and avoiding serious risks" — [Wikipedia: Chief investment officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_investment_officer)

### Inferences
- For a decision-ledger positioned on compliance and AI-governance value, the sponsor most likely to have 2026 budget and urgency is the CCO (backed by the IAA/ACA numbers), with the COO / Head of Operations as co-sponsor for workflow (four-eyes, exceptions, corporate actions). The investment CIO and PMs are users whose veto is behavioural ("will this slow me down?") rather than budgetary. This is inference from the survey data, not a sourced buyer map.
- The Deloitte "central AI team with enterprisewide authority" is the de facto evaluation and veto body for anything with an LLM in it; a vendor should expect to be assessed against that team's artifact list (model inventory entry, model risk assessment, lineage, HITL thresholds, audit trail, incident response) in addition to InfoSec's DDQ.
- Reg S-P's service-provider oversight duty (fully in force from June 2026 for all SEC advisers) gives Legal/Compliance a formal reason to veto a vendor that cannot evidence controls; hedge fund advisers registered with the SEC fall under the same duty.
- Hedge funds versus long-only: no retrieved source distinguishes their buying structures; the safest working assumption is that at hedge funds the COO/CTO and CCO sit closer to the founder-PM and can sign faster, whereas at USD 5-100bn long-only managers procurement, InfoSec and a formal vendor-risk function are separate gates (inference only).

## Key Question 2: Procurement and due diligence — DDQ contents, certifications, DORA/FCA outsourcing rules, data residency, LLM-API questions, and review timelines

### Takeaway
A vendor selling to alternative managers should expect the AITEC-AIMA Illustrative Questionnaire for the Due Diligence of Vendor Technology and Cyber Security (used by 250+ managers and 200+ vendors) plus a SOC 2 Type II with a recent observation period; EU buyers will require DORA Article 30 clauses (written single contract, SLAs, incident assistance, termination rights, audit access, subcontracting consent, and for critical/important functions a tested exit plan), UK buyers the SYSC 8 written-agreement and access-rights terms; AI-specific diligence now centres on training-use, data residency, sub-processor (OpenAI/Anthropic) disclosure, zero-retention and how non-deterministic outputs are logged and audited.

### Cited Findings
- AITEC created a vendor DDQ in 2014, later partnered with AIMA and rebranded it the "AITEC-AIMA Illustrative Questionnaire for the Due Diligence of Vendor Technology and Cyber Security"; it is "a superset of technology questions compiled by a committee of AITEC members who are senior technologists in the financial services industry", supports vendor comparison "through risk scoring", and has been adopted by over 250 alternative asset management firms and 200 global vendors — [AITEC DDQ page](https://www.aitec.org/ddq); [AIMA due diligence questionnaires](https://www.aima.org/sound-practices/due-diligence-questionnaires.html); [AITEC "Become a Vendor"](https://www.aitec.org/vendors); [Vendor Questionnaire Library entry for the AIMA DDQ](https://vendorquestionnaires.com/q/aima-ddq)
- AIMA released an updated AITEC-AIMA DDQ for Vendor Technology and Cyber Security and separately solicited input to shape its 2025 DDQ — [AIMA: launches new due diligence template](https://www.aima.org/article/aima-launches-new-due-diligence-template.html); [AIMA: help shape AIMA's 2025 DDQ](https://www.aima.org/article/help-shape-aima-s-2025-due-diligence-questionnaire.html)
- An illustrative cyber DDQ for vendors is also circulated in PE/hedge fund events material — [PEI Events: Cybersecurity DDQ for Your Vendors (2019 PDF)](https://www.peievents.com/en/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Cybersecurity-DDQ-for-Your-Vendors.pdf)
- DORA Article 30 "mandates specific clauses that must be present in all ICT third-party service contracts, with additional requirements for contracts supporting critical or important functions"; all contracts must be "set out in writing in a single document" with a baseline including data-protection provisions, service-level descriptions, termination rights and incident-assistance obligations — [Vendorica: DORA Article 30 explained](https://vendorica.com/dora/third-party-risk/article-30/); [Springlex Article 30 text](https://www.springlex.eu/en/packages/dora/dora-regulation/article-30/); [Luxgap Art. 30 official text + guide](https://luxgap.com/lois/dora/art-30/)
- DORA exit strategies for critical/important functions must include transition plans with timelines, data portability and migration provisions, identification of alternative providers, contractually defined transition periods, testing of exit procedures and stakeholder communication plans, with "a mandatory adequate transition period during which the provider continues to supply the service" — [Glocert: DORA ICT third-party risk, contracting and exit](https://www.glocertinternational.com/resources/guides/dora-ict-third-party-risk-contracting-and-exit/); [DORApp: Exit Strategy DORA (2026)](https://blog.dorapp.eu/dora-compliance/exit-strategy-dora/)
- Contracts must carry "explicit authorization (or prohibition) of subcontracting with its conditions"; subcontracting controls are often sharpened "into a specific veto right over the addition of a US-headquartered sub-processor, which DORA permits and which the ESAs reinforced through the March 2025 amendments to the subcontracting technical standard"; right-to-audit, exit strategy and sub-contracting controls are "mandatory clauses you cannot negotiate out" — [CyAdviso: DORA third-party ICT risk, Articles 28-30 guide for 2026](https://www.cyadviso.com/dora-third-party-ict-risk); [OSDomains: DORA Article 30 contract terms](https://osdomains.com/regulations/dora); [Regulation-DORA.eu supplier guide](https://www.regulation-dora.eu/blog/dora-third-party-ict-providers-guide-for-suppliers)
- Atlant Security frames DORA for SaaS companies around Article 30, critical-provider designation and a "SOC 2 gap" (i.e. SOC 2 alone does not satisfy DORA's contractual and resilience demands) — [Atlant Security: DORA for SaaS](https://atlantsecurity.com/blog/dora-for-saas-companies) (title-level evidence; article not fetched)
- FCA SYSC 8: a firm "must ensure that it takes reasonable steps to avoid undue operational risk" and cannot outsource functions in a way that impairs internal controls or the regulator's ability to monitor compliance; where a third party including a cloud provider delivers services on behalf of a regulated firm this is outsourcing and "the specific form of the service, whether public, private or hybrid cloud, or IaaS, PaaS or SaaS, does not change the regulatory obligations"; cloud services supporting important business functions are "potentially material outsourcing, triggering enhanced due diligence" — [FCA Handbook SYSC 8.1](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/sysc8/sysc8s1); [Baker McKenzie: rules for cloud outsourcing, UK](https://resourcehub.bakermckenzie.com/en/resources/cloud-compliance-center/emea/united-kingdom/topics/rules-for-cloud-outsourcing); [Compare the Cloud: FCA operational resilience guide 2025](https://www.comparethecloud.net/articles/uk-fintech-cloud-compliance-fca-operational-resilience-2025)
- SYSC 8.1.9R requires rights and obligations to be "clearly allocated and set out in a written agreement"; the firm must keep "its instruction and termination rights, its rights of information, and its right to inspections and access to books and premises", and sub-outsourcing may occur only with the firm's written consent; a firm outsourcing a critical or important function "remains fully responsible for discharging all of its obligations under the regulatory system" — [LexisNexis UK: financial services outsourcing guidance](https://www.lexisnexis.com/en-gb/legal/guidance/financial-services-outsourcing); [Nasara Connect: FCA SYSC 8 rules guide](https://nasaraconnect.com/blog/outsourcing-and-third-party-risk-management-the-fca-rules-explained); [Google Cloud SYSC 8 mapping](https://cloud.google.com/security/compliance/sysc_8_outsourcing_fca_handbook_gcp_mapping)
- SOC 2 expectations from adviser buyers: "a SOC 2 Type II report with the Security, Confidentiality, and Privacy criteria, an unqualified opinion, and an observation period that ended within the last 12 months" — [Zocks](https://www.zocks.io/blog/soc-2-compliance-for-financial-advisors-what-you-need-to-know)
- Buyer-side vendor review starts by asking whether the vendor has a SOC 2 report or ISO 27001 certification and reviewing it for red flags — [Secureleap: SOC 2 vendor management](https://www.secureleap.tech/blog/soc-2-vendor-management-for-startups); [Scrut: reviewing vendor SOC 2 reports](https://www.scrut.io/hub/soc-2/review-vendor-soc-2-report)
- Startups "pursue SOC 2 to unblock enterprise procurement and vendor security reviews, not to satisfy a single regulator"; most start with Type I then Type II within 6-12 months — [Atlant Security: SOC 2 for startups](https://atlantsecurity.com/blog/soc-2-for-startups); [Promise Legal SOC 2 roadmap 2025](https://promise.legal/guides/soc2-roadmap); [Folksoft SOC 2 guide 2026](https://blog.folksoft.tech/posts/soc-2-for-startups-guide-2026)
- AI-vendor diligence: three essential questions are "Will our data be used to train your AI models?", "Where is our data processed and stored?", "What third-party AI services do you use?" — [TrustArc: AI supply chain risk](https://trustarc.com/resource/ai-supply-chain-risk-vendor-due-diligence/)
- Buyers should verify "zero-retention" policies for inference data and ask whether a prompt "is stored or used to retrain their base model" — [BRICS-Econ: TPRM for vendors handling LLM data, 2026 guide](https://brics-econ.org/third-party-risk-management-for-vendors-handling-llm-data-a-2026-guide); [Atlas Systems: AI vendor risk questionnaire 2026](https://www.atlassystems.com/blog/ai-vendor-risk-questionnaire)
- "Many AI vendors use commercial LLM APIs from OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google Gemini ... user data may be transmitted to these APIs without explicit contracts"; whether a vendor "runs OpenAI as primary and Anthropic as failover versus only one provider has different audit, residency, and incident response implications" — [DeepInspect: 27-question AI vendor DD checklist](https://www.deepinspect.ai/blog/ai-vendor-due-diligence-checklist); [FirmAdapt: vendor security questionnaire that catches AI risk](https://firmadapt.com/blog/vendor-security-questionnaire-catches-ai-risk)
- AI-specific questions also cover training-data provenance, "model retraining frequency and notification of parameter changes", and whether vendors "can explain why specific decisions were made"; "AI outputs are non-deterministic ... for regulated industries, how this is handled, logged, and audited is a material risk" — [Ampcus Cyber: AI vendor DDQ](https://www.ampcuscyber.com/blogs/third-party-ai-vendor-due-diligence-questionnaire/); [Worqlo: enterprise AI vendor RFP, 40 questions (2026)](https://worqlo.com/blog/enterprise-ai-vendor-rfp-questions/); [TechTarget: CIO's guide to LLM risk management](https://www.techtarget.com/searchcio/tip/Beyond-thehypeA-CIOs-guide-to-LLM-risk-management)
- Timelines: "The average sales cycle in regulated sectors like financial services ranges from 150-240 days, where security and compliance review dominate the second half of the deal"; one fintech selling to enterprise FIs had "average sales cycles of 14.6 months ... with technical validation processes consuming 4+ months per prospect"; "security and compliance reviews frequently restart due to inadequate preparation" — [ORM: sales cycle length benchmarks](https://orm-tech.com/blog/sales-cycle-length-guide/); [Forth & Scale case study: shortening fintech sales cycles by 47%](https://www.forthandscale.com/case-studies/accelerating-financial-sales) (attribution of individual figures approximate)

### Inferences
- Document pack a vendor must have ready before first InfoSec contact (derived from the sources above): completed AITEC-AIMA DDQ (or SIG-equivalent) with risk-score-ready answers; SOC 2 Type II (Security + Confidentiality + Privacy, observation period ending inside 12 months) or a dated Type I plus Type II timetable; latest penetration-test summary; ISO 27001 status; data-residency and hosting-region statement; sub-processor list naming every LLM provider and its role (primary/failover), with the provider's zero-retention and no-training terms attached; DORA Article 30-ready contract template (single document, SLAs, incident-assistance, termination, audit/access rights, subcontracting consent, exit plan with transition period and tested procedures, TLPT participation clause); SYSC 8-ready terms (instruction/termination/information/inspection rights, sub-outsourcing consent); model-risk documentation (model inventory entry, change-notification policy, non-determinism handling, decision explainability, HITL thresholds); incident-response and BC/DR plans; register-of-information data fields for the client's DORA register.
- A decision ledger will often be classified by EU/UK clients as supporting a "critical or important function" (it gates investment decisions and holds regulatory evidence), which pulls in the heavier DORA Art. 30(3) and SYSC 8 material-outsourcing tier; a vendor should plan for the heavy tier rather than argue for the light one.
- Because gnosis logs every chat-agent tool call as lineage and exposes only read-only tools to the agent, the vendor can answer the "non-determinism, logging and audit" and "explain why the decision was made" questions structurally; this should be made a first-page item in the DDQ response rather than buried.
- Review timing for a startup: budget the full InfoSec/legal/DORA track at roughly the second half of a 150-240-day cycle (i.e. 3-4 months), and assume it restarts if any document is missing — consistent with both the ORM benchmark and the Forth & Scale "4+ months technical validation" example.

## Key Question 3: Pricing models used by comparable vendors, public numbers, and what fits a compliance-valued decision ledger

### Takeaway
Public evidence shows three live models: AUM-basis-point subscription (Clearwater, ~95% subscription revenue, secondary sources put typical fees at 0.5-2 bps), ARR subscription with high net dollar retention (Enfusion NDR 122.4% at June 2021, 98% recurring revenue) and per-seat data terminals (Bloomberg ~USD 31,980/seat/year in 2026 per aggregator sites); Bipsync, the closest workflow comparable, publishes no pricing.

### Cited Findings
- Clearwater Analytics derives "approximately 95 percent of revenue from subscriptions, mainly priced in basis points on client AUM"; "a typical client will pay fees in the range of 0.5 to 2 basis points annually on total assets administered — on a $10 billion portfolio that translates to roughly $500,000 to $2 million per year" — [MatrixBCG: how Clearwater works](https://matrixbcg.com/blogs/how-it-works/clearwateranalytics); [KoalaGains CWAN profile](https://koalagains.com/stocks/NYSE/CWAN) (secondary analyst-blog sources; the bps range is not from a Clearwater filing)
- Clearwater's net revenue retention was 108% in Q3 2025, down from 110% in Q2, "attributed to lower AUM growth and lapping large September 2025 wins"; core business NRR was 114% as of 30 June 2025; NRR is defined to include "changes from the addition, removal, or value of assets on our platform, contractual changes ... and lost revenue from client attrition" — [Motley Fool Q3 2025 call transcript](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/04/14/clearwater-cwan-q3-2025-earnings-call-transcript/); [Clearwater Q2 2025 results](https://cwan.com/press-releases/clearwater-analytics-announces-second-quarter-2025-financial-results/); [Clearwater Q1 2025 8-K exhibit](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1866368/000162828025021074/cwan-20250331xexx991.htm); [Clearwater FY2025 results exhibit](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1866368/000186636826000009/cwan-20251231xexx991.htm)
- Enfusion S-1 (filed September 2021): net dollar retention 122.4% as of 30 June 2021 versus 109.8% a year earlier; FY2020 NDR 119.6% versus 111.3% in FY2019; ARR USD 107.9m at 30 June 2021 versus USD 74.9m; recurring subscription revenue is 98% of total revenue; ARR is calculated monthly by annualising platform subscription and managed-services revenue recognised in the last month of the period — [Enfusion Form S-1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1868912/000155837021012603/tmb-20210917xs1.htm); [Blossom Street Ventures: learnings from the Enfusion IPO](https://blossomstreetventures.medium.com/learnings-from-a-saas-ipo-enfusion-5df9d8e497fa); [Seeking Alpha on Enfusion](https://seekingalpha.com/article/4520068-enfusion-financial-saas-company-buy)
- Bloomberg Terminal 2026: USD 31,980 per year for a single seat, USD 28,320 per seat for multi-terminal clients, reflecting a 6.5% increase on contracts renewing from January 2025; volume deals reportedly USD 20,000-22,000 per terminal; two-year contracts 3-5% discount, three-year 5-8% — [CostBench Bloomberg pricing](https://costbench.com/software/financial-data-terminals/bloomberg-terminal/); [Godel Discount: Bloomberg cost 2026](https://godeldiscount.com/blog/bloomberg-terminal-cost-2026); [Abloomify: Bloomberg cost](https://www.abloomify.com/blog/bloomberg-terminal-cost); [Vendr Bloomberg marketplace page](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/bloomberg) (all secondary aggregators; Bloomberg does not publish list prices)
- Bipsync publishes no pricing; Capterra, G2, GetApp and Software Advice all list "contact vendor"; Bipsync describes itself as an RMS "proven to enhance investor productivity, boost fund collaboration and simplify compliance" for "investment management firms of all sizes" — [Capterra Bipsync](https://www.capterra.com/p/138677/Bipsync/); [G2 Bipsync pricing](https://www.g2.com/products/bipsync/pricing); [Bipsync for asset managers](https://bipsync.com/asset-managers/)
- Gartner forecasts spending on AI governance platforms of USD 492m in 2026, surpassing USD 1bn by 2030, driven by fragmented AI regulation projected to extend to 75% of the world's economies by 2030 — [Gartner press release, 17 Feb 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-02-17-gartner-global-ai-regulations-fuel-billion-dollar-market-for-ai-governance-platforms)

### Inferences
- AUM-basis-point pricing works for Clearwater because its work (accounting/reporting on every position) scales with assets; a decision ledger's cost-to-serve scales with decision volume, decision types and approver seats, not AUM, so bps pricing would be hard to defend to a CFO and would under-monetise hedge funds with high decision velocity and low AUM. Preferred structure (inference): an annual platform fee (covering the event store, policy engine, audit log, MCP server) plus per-decision-type modules (guideline exception, corporate-action election, IC decision, model-change sign-off) plus a per-named-approver/PM seat band, with LLM-dependent features (chat agent, recommendation engine) metered or capped separately so that InfoSec can switch them off without touching the ledger price. Enfusion's 98% recurring/high-NDR pattern suggests module expansion is the growth engine to design for.
- Bloomberg's ~USD 32k/seat is a useful anchor only for user-facing research tools; a compliance-valued ledger should anchor instead to the cost of a compliance FTE or an external audit/exam finding (no sourced numbers for those this session).
- Absence of public pricing at Bipsync and the other RMS/decision-tool vendors implies buyers in this segment expect bespoke quotes; publishing an indicative price band could be a differentiator for a startup with no references (inference).

## Key Question 4: Segment prioritisation — which segment closes fastest and why (hedge funds, long-only, multi-manager, wealth, OCIO, family offices, administrators, asset owners)

### Inferences
- Priority 1 (inference): US SEC-registered advisers in the USD 5-50bn band — both multi-fund long-only managers and multi-PM hedge funds — because the 2026 compliance-priority data is the only sourced, time-bound "why now", and gnosis's demo arc (5 funds, 5 PMs, CIO) mirrors that structure.
