## Key Question 1: Institutional finance UI conventions (density, keyboard-first, command palettes, numerals, colour semantics, dark mode, multi-monitor) and published design systems

### Takeaway
The best-documented institutional systems (J.P. Morgan Salt, IBM Carbon, Bloomberg Terminal) converge on: token-driven density modes with a high-density option explicitly reserved for expert, data-heavy screens; keyboard-primary interaction so that the visible field is content rather than navigation chrome; light and dark modes that change only colour tokens; and status colour that is never the sole carrier of meaning. Salt is the only public finance design system that states the trade-off numerically (high density: 20 px control height; fails WCAG 2.2 AA target size; must be an informed opt-in), which makes it the best reference for a "clinical but compliant" density policy.

### Inferences
- A defensible density policy for gnosis: default to a Salt-style "medium" (28 px controls, 32–40 px table rows) that passes WCAG 2.2 AA; offer "high" (20–24 px rows, 12 px icons) as an explicit, per-user opt-in with an accessibility notice, and never remove function when density changes. This mirrors Salt's documented governance and Carbon's row-height ladder.
- "Clinical" in the Bloomberg sense is achieved less by colour or type than by removing navigation chrome and putting navigation into the keyboard (command palette + mnemonic shortcuts); the visible surface should be almost entirely data and states.
- Numeric columns should use lining tabular figures (`font-variant-numeric: lining-nums tabular-nums`) and right alignment; Salt does not document this, so it is a differentiator rather than table stakes.
- Compliance-state colour must be a five-level scale (pass / info / warn / breach / hard-block) that pairs colour with a distinct icon and label, at 3:1 contrast between adjacent states; the "hard gate cannot be overridden" state should differ in shape (e.g., lock glyph) and in available actions, not only hue.

## Key Question 2: Decision-support and explainable-AI interface guidelines (HAX, PAIR, IBM, NIST), rationale/provenance/confidence patterns, and reducing automation bias

### Takeaway
The four canonical guideline sets agree that a decision-support UI must state what the system can and cannot do, show how reliable it is, expose why it acted, degrade or refuse when uncertain, and make correction cheap. The empirical literature adds a hard constraint: explanations alone increase acceptance of AI output "regardless of its correctness" (Bansal et al. 2021), while cognitive forcing functions (decide first, reveal on demand, or delay) measurably reduce overreliance at the cost of user preference (Buçinca et al. 2021). For hard gates and four-eyes approvals, this argues for deliberate friction, not just better explanations.

### Cited Findings

**Microsoft HAX Guidelines (Amershi et al., CHI 2019)**
- Microsoft's 18 Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction are "a synthesis of more than 20 years' research", introduced in a 2019 CHI paper, organised by phase: initially, during interaction, when wrong, over time — [Microsoft HAX Toolkit, Guidelines](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/haxtoolkit/ai-guidelines/)
- The guidelines were validated in a study with "49 design practitioners assessing 20 AI-infused products" — [Amershi et al., Microsoft Research publication page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/) (sample figures quoted via mirrors: [InterfaceDNA reflection](https://github.com/aborle3201/InterfaceDNA-IS492/blob/main/reflections/aabha_borle.md); DOI 10.1145/3290605.3300233)
- Full text of the 18 (mirror of the official wording): G1 "Make clear what the system can do"; G2 "Make clear how well the system can do what it can do"; G3 "Time services based on context"; G4 "Show contextually relevant information"; G5 "Match relevant social norms"; G6 "Mitigate social biases"; G7 "Support efficient invocation"; G8 "Support efficient dismissal"; G9 "Support efficient correction"; G10 "Scope services when in doubt" ("Engage in disambiguation or gracefully degrade the AI system's services when uncertain"); G11 "Make clear why the system did what it did"; G12 "Remember recent interactions"; G13 "Learn from user behavior"; G14 "Update and adapt cautiously"; G15 "Encourage granular feedback"; G16 "Convey the consequences of user actions"; G17 "Provide global controls"; G18 "Notify users about changes" — [HAX guideline list (mirror)](https://github.com/Quirinevwm/qs-design-casino/blob/main/skills/human-ai-interaction-review.md); canonical: [HAX Design Library](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/haxtoolkit/library/)

**Google PAIR People + AI Guidebook**

- Chapter structure (mirror summary): "Help users calibrate their trust"; "Plan for trust calibration throughout the product experience"; "Optimize for understanding" (show data sources, model confidence, example-based and interactive explanations); "Manage influence on user decisions"; confidence can be shown as categorical, N-best alternatives, or numeric — [PAIR Guidebook summary (mirror)](https://github.com/siriyaoff/siriyaoff.github.io/blob/master/_posts/2022-08-10-Misc-Google-People+AI-Guidebook.md); guidebook launch post: [Google Design, People + AI Guidebook](https://medium.com/google-design/people-ai-guidebook-41ec2ee5ec3f)

**IBM Carbon for AI and IBM Design for AI**

**NIST IR 8312 Four Principles of Explainable AI (2021)**
- Four principles: systems deliver "accompanying evidence or reasons for outcomes and processes" (Explanation); explanations are "understandable to individual users" (Meaningful); explanations "correctly reflect the system's process for generating the output" (Explanation Accuracy); and "a system only operates under conditions for which it was designed and when it reaches sufficient confidence in its output" (Knowledge Limits) — [NISTIR 8312 PDF](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2021/nist.ir.8312.pdf) (snippet only; blocked); DOI [10.6028/NIST.IR.8312](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.IR.8312); authors P. **Empirical evidence on explanations, overreliance and cognitive forcing**
- Bansal et al. (CHI 2021) abstract: "While we observed complementary improvements from AI augmentation, they were not increased by explanations. Rather, explanations increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness." Studies ran on three datasets with an AI "with accuracy comparable to humans" — [arXiv 2006.14779](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14779); [ACM DOI 10.1145/3411764.3445717](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3411764.3445717) (abstract text verified via mirror: [goldstandard-reviewer-paper-match corpus](https://github.com/niharshah/goldstandard-reviewer-paper-match))
- Buçinca, Malaya & Gajos (CSCW 2021): compared three cognitive forcing functions ("on-demand" — show AI only when clicked; "update" — user decides first, then sees AI; "wait" — delay before AI is shown) against two simple XAI conditions (explanation, uncertainty) and a no-AI baseline in a nutrition task; "cognitive forcing functions significantly reduced overreliance compared to simple explainable AI approaches"; "people assigned the least favorable subjective ratings to the" most effective designs; benefits were "disproportionately greater for individuals with high Need for Cognition", a potential "intervention-generated inequality" — [arXiv 2102.09692](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09692); [ACM DOI 10.1145/3449287](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3449287); [Harvard PDF](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2021/bucinca21trust.pdf) (all blocked; findings via search summary and mirror summaries [lit_git](https://github.com/tegorman13/lit_git/blob/main/HAI/summaries/Bu%C3%A7inca_2021_02030829.md))
- Follow-on work: "Cognitive Forcing for Better Decision-Making: Reducing Overreliance on AI Systems Through Partial Explanations", PACM HCI 2025 — [ACM DOI 10.1145/3710946](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3710946) (title only); "Impacts of cognitive forcing and need for cognition on biased AI-assisted decision making about mental health emergencies", Scientific Reports 2025 — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-30506-3) (title only)
- Automation bias foundations: Parasuraman & Manzey, "Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation", Human Factors 52(3), 2010 — [DOI 10.1177/0018720810376055](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055); a mirror quotes it as finding that acceptance of wrong machine output "cannot be prevented by training or instructions" — [lokf-agent-skills notes (mirror; verify wording)](https://github.com/noelmcloughlin/lokf-agent-skills/blob/main/docs/three-lines-critics.md). - Green (2022) argues policies requiring human oversight of algorithms rest on people who "are unable to perform the desired oversight functions" — [Computer Law & Security Review, DOI 10.1016/j.clsr.2022.105681](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clsr.2022.105681) (quote via mirror above)

**Uncertainty visualisation**
- Padilla, Kay & Hullman (2022): distributional displays (quantile dotplots, hypothetical outcome plots) that use frequency framing outperform interval plots; best practice is to show distributional information "in a way that does not allow the viewer to fixate on summary information such as a mean"; Hullman found visualisation authors reluctant to show quantified uncertainty for fear of losing credibility — [Uncertainty Visualization, Wiley StatsRef](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781118445112.stat08296) (snippet only); PDF: [UC Merced](http://space.ucmerced.edu/Downloads/publications/Uncertainty_Visualization_Padilla_Kay_Hullman_2022.pdf) (blocked)
- Padilla et al., "Uncertain About Uncertainty: How Qualitative Expressions of Forecaster Confidence Impact Decision-Making With Uncertainty Visualizations", Frontiers in Psychology 2021 — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.579267/full) (title only)

**Agent trace transparency**
- 2026 survey: "Trustworthy tool use requires tracing why a tool was selected, where its arguments came from, whether its output was reliable, and how the result shaped later decisions"; provenance should "connect sources, arguments, outputs, and downstream consumers into a single dependency chain"; "execution provenance is the typed graph of everything an agent did, and evidence tracing is its projection onto which evidence supported which claim" — [From Agent Traces to Trust, arXiv 2606.04990](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04990) (blocked; snippet plus mirror notes [agent_debugger](https://github.com/acailic/agent_debugger/blob/main/docs/papers/from-agent-traces-to-trust-provenance-survey.md))

### Inferences
- Map gnosis screens to guidelines: recommendation panel = HAX G2 (show reliability), G10 (scope/refuse when uncertain), G11 (why); precedent panel with matcher attribution = NIST "Explanation Accuracy" (the shown weights must be the actual matcher weights, not a post-hoc story); hard gates and kill-switch = NIST "Knowledge Limits" made visible; chat agent with read-only tools = HAX G1 (what it can do) and the provenance survey's tool-selection/argument/output chain.
- Given Bansal and Buçinca, the recommendation should not be the first thing a PM sees on a high-stakes decision: a "record your view first, then reveal" (update-CFF) or "reveal on request" (on-demand-CFF) step at the approval/exception stage is the evidence-backed way to reduce rubber-stamping, accepting that users will rate it less pleasant. Apply it only to gated/high-impact decision types to limit friction and NFC-driven inequality.
- Carbon's rule that AI styling disappears once a human edits maps directly onto "validated prose": AI-drafted rationale should carry an AI label until a human edits or approves it, after which the label changes to a human-attributed state, with an explicit revert control.
- Confidence for precedents should be shown as N-best with numeric similarity (PAIR's "N-best" plus "numeric" forms) and, where a distribution exists (e.g., outcome spread across precedents), as a frequency-framed dot plot rather than a single mean.
- Refusals and short-circuits (hard gate hit, tool error, out-of-scope question) are first-class states: NIST Knowledge Limits and HAX G10 both require the UI to say that the system stopped and why, rather than degrading silently.

## Key Question 3: Approval and audit UX (four-eyes, audit timelines, diffs, immutable records, as-of time travel, sealed vs mutable state)

### Takeaway
Banking maker-checker practice supplies the state model (pending record visible but inactive; submitted payload frozen; cancel-and-resubmit instead of edit; every step logged with identity and time), and bitemporal databases supply the two clocks the UI must expose (valid time = "what was true", system time = "what we knew"). The most concrete public UI precedents for as-of controls are XTDB's finance tutorial (paired valid-time and system-time sliders) and GOV.UK's "check answers" page as the review-before-seal step.

### Cited Findings

**Four-eyes / maker-checker**
- Maker-checker "is one of the central principles of authorization in the information systems of financial organizations", requiring "at least two individuals" per transaction: "one individual may create a transaction, the other individual should be involved in confirmation/authorization" — [Maker-checker, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maker-checker) (snippet only; blocked)
- Implementation guidance: the maker submits and "the system places the record in a pending state — visible to authorized reviewers but not yet active"; "store the exact payload at submission time. Never allow modification of a pending request's payload; require cancellation and re-submission instead"; use fine-grained MAKER and CHECKER roles — [Opcito, Maker-Checker implementation guide](https://www.opcito.com/blogs/maker-checker-implementation-guide-for-secure-fintech-systems) (snippet only)
- Audit trail expectation: "Every action—from the maker's initiation to the checker's review and the final decision—is logged with timestamps and user identities", answering "who, what, when, and why" — [XTRM, Maker-Checker Process](https://blog.xtrm.com/posts/maker-checker-process) (snippet only)
- Four-eyes explainer with regulatory framing — [ChequeDB, Four-Eyes Principle](https://chequedb.com/resources/blog/four-eyes-principle-foundations) (blocked; not verified)

**Bitemporal "as-of" model and controls**
- XTDB: "System Time" tracks when the database became aware of changes (immutable, automatic); "Valid Time" is when facts are considered true in the real world (user-editable); "System time alone doesn't solve all temporal problems—it's often necessary to additionally track the time period where these facts are considered valid in the real world" — [XTDB, Time in XTDB (GitHub source)](https://github.com/xtdb/xtdb/blob/main/docs/src/content/docs/about/time-in-xtdb.md)
- SQL:2011 syntax XTDB exposes: `FOR VALID_TIME AS OF DATE '...'`, `FOR VALID_TIME BETWEEN ... AND ...`, `FOR ALL VALID_TIME`; `FOR SYSTEM_TIME AS OF ...`, `FOR SYSTEM_TIME BETWEEN ...`, `FOR ALL SYSTEM_TIME`; session defaults via `SETTING DEFAULT VALID_TIME AS OF ..., DEFAULT SYSTEM_TIME AS OF ...` — [Time in XTDB](https://github.com/xtdb/xtdb/blob/main/docs/src/content/docs/about/time-in-xtdb.md); [XTDB SQL overview](https://github.com/xtdb/xtdb/blob/main/docs/src/content/docs/quickstart/sql-overview.mdx)
- The two clocks answer different questions: "Most use cases will want to see corrections—they don't care that data arrived late" (valid time); "Some use cases (e.g. auditing) will need to see data 'as we knew it at the time,' without subsequent corrections" (system time) — [Time in XTDB](https://github.com/xtdb/xtdb/blob/main/docs/src/content/docs/about/time-in-xtdb.md)
- XTDB finance tutorial uses trades as the example, with interactive date-range sliders for valid time and system time so users can query "as of" a valid date, adjust the system-time snapshot to see how corrections appear, and preview future-dated changes; "a full audit history is maintained automatically, and the history of the entire database can be 'replayed' with microsecond granularity"; "software engineers working in financial domains are forced to routinely confront this fundamental 'mutability' across their database systems to ensure that sufficient records are kept for satisfying auditors and regulators" — [XTDB, Time in Finance tutorial](https://github.com/xtdb/xtdb/blob/main/docs/src/content/docs/tutorials/financial-usecase/time-in-finance.mdx)
- XTDB positions itself for "time-travel reporting and data compliance": "you can audit the full history of your database at any point, without needing snapshots or accessing backups" — [xtdb/xtdb README](https://github.com/xtdb/xtdb)
- Bitemporality is "a critical requirement for systems in any industry with strong auditing regulations, where you need to be able to answer the question 'what did you know and when did you know it?'" — [XTDB v1 docs, Bitemporality](https://v1-docs.xtdb.com/concepts/bitemporality/); [JUXT, FAQs on Bitemporality](https://www.juxt.pro/blog/bitemporal-webinar-q-and-a/) (snippets only)

**Review-before-commit and confirmation**
- GOV.UK "Check answers" pattern: "Show a single check answers page immediately before the confirmation screen for small to medium-sized transactions"; it builds confidence that all sections are complete and "reduces errors by giving users a final opportunity to catch and fix mistakes"; include "Change" links per row (with hidden descriptive text for screen readers), pre-populate on revisit, show only relevant sections, mark skipped optional answers as "Not provided", and word the submit button specifically (e.g., "Send your claim form") — [GOV.UK Design System, Check answers (GitHub source)](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-design-system/blob/main/src/patterns/check-answers/index.md); canonical: [design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/check-answers](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/check-answers/)
- For very large multi-section transactions, GOV.UK suggests a check-answers page at the end of each section, "particularly valuable when different users complete separate sections" — [GOV.UK Check answers](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-design-system/blob/main/src/patterns/check-answers/index.md)
- GOV.UK "Complete multiple tasks" pattern adds a per-task "Have you completed this section?" question, including for "answers that need to be checked carefully with someone else" — [GOV.UK Complete multiple tasks](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-design-system/blob/main/src/patterns/complete-multiple-tasks/index.md)

**AI content in audited flows**
- Carbon for AI: AI-generated content must carry an AI label; the label is dropped when a human overrides the content and a "revert to AI" control is offered — [Carbon for AI](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/main/src/pages/guidelines/carbon-for-ai/index.mdx)

### Inferences
- Gnosis's approvals should adopt the maker-checker state machine literally: Proposed → Pending approval (payload frozen, visibly read-only, "cancel and resubmit" as the only edit path) → Approved/Rejected → Sealed. The frozen-payload rule is what makes a diff view trustworthy: the diff compares immutable snapshots, never a live form.
- The as-of replay control should be two explicit clocks, labelled in plain language ("Facts as of" = valid time; "Known as of" = system time), defaulting to now/now, with a persistent banner whenever either clock is not "now" so a user cannot mistake a replay for the current state. XTDB's paired sliders are the closest public precedent.
- Sealed vs mutable state needs at least two redundant cues (icon + label + disabled affordances), consistent with Carbon's "two of colour, shape, symbol" rule; a sealed record should show the seal time on the system-time clock and who approved it.
- The commit/seal step should be a GOV.UK-style check-answers page: per-fund implications, gate results, approver identities, and change links back to the originating step, with a specific button label ("Seal decision D-1234 for 3 funds") rather than "Submit".

Collapsed by default, expandable, and copyable into the decision's audit record.

## Key Question 5: Numeric benchmarks (response time, Core Web Vitals, accessibility law, usability metrics, NPS)

### Takeaway
The verifiable 2026 benchmark set is: interaction feedback within 100 ms and task flow within 1 s (Nielsen); INP ≤ 200 ms, LCP ≤ 2.5 s, CLS ≤ 0.1 at the 75th percentile (Google); WCAG 2.2 AA including 24×24 px targets (W3C), with EN 301 549 v4.1.1 (Sept 2026) now pointing at WCAG 2.2 and Section 508 still anchored to WCAG 2.0 AA; SUS ≥ 68 is average and ≥ 80.8 is an "A"; a 78% task-completion rate is average and > 92% is top quartile; B2B SaaS NPS clusters in the mid-30s to low-40s. No credible finance-software-specific SUS or CSAT benchmark was found.

### Cited Findings

**Response time**
- "0.1 second is about the limit for having the user feel that the system is reacting instantaneously, meaning that no special feedback is necessary except to display the result. 1.0 second is about the limit for the user's flow of thought to stay uninterrupted, even though the user will notice the delay. Normally, no special feedback is necessary during delays of more than 0.1 but less than 1.0 second, but the user does lose the feeling of operating directly on the data" — [NN/g, Response Times: The 3 Important Limits](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/) (blocked; wording verified via [IBM Watson design guide mirror](https://github.com/IBM-Watson/design-guide/blob/master/guides/02-guidelines/user-experience/performance/index.md)). The third limit (about 10 seconds for keeping attention on the dialogue) is on the same page but was not visible in the mirrored fragment.

**Core Web Vitals (Google)**
- INP: good ≤ 200 ms, needs improvement 200–500 ms, poor > 500 ms, measured at "the 75th percentile of page loads recorded in the field, segmented across mobile and desktop devices"; INP was announced to "replace First Input Delay (FID) in March 2024" — [web.dev INP article (GitHub source, updated June 2023)](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/web.dev/blob/main/src/site/content/en/metrics/inp/index.md); canonical [web.dev/articles/inp](https://web.dev/articles/inp)

## Key Question 6: Data-table and form best practice for expert users (NN/g, Baymard, inline validation, progressive disclosure, schema-driven forms, wizards vs single page)

### Takeaway
NN/g's definition of complex applications fits gnosis exactly (highly trained users, unstructured goals, high-impact decisions), and its guidance is to organise rather than remove complexity, validate inline as soon as a field is completed, and reserve wizards for unfamiliar or rare processes while giving repeat users a faster single-surface path. Evidence that multi-step wizards reduce abandonment is consumer-conversion folklore and should not be applied to expert PM workflows.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g defines complex applications as "any application supporting the broad, unstructured goals or nonlinear workflows of highly trained users in specialized domains", explicitly including "systems supporting high-impact or high-value decision making"; "even users of complex applications tend to plateau at mediocre performance, and the majority of users do not transition to true expert usage... when left to their own devices" — [NN/g, 8 Design Guidelines for Complex Applications](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/complex-application-design/); [NN/g, UX Strategies for Complex-Application Design](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/strategies-complex-application-design/) (snippets only; blocked)
- NN/g's ten heuristics applied to complex applications — [NN/g, 10 Usability Heuristics Applied to Complex Applications](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-heuristics-complex-applications/) (title only)
- Data tables should support four user tasks: "find records that fit specific criteria, compare data, view/edit/add a single row's data, and take actions on records" — [NN/g, Data Tables: Four Major User Tasks (LinkedIn summary of NN/g video)](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/nielsen-norman-group_data-tables-four-major-user-tasks-activity-6917470288167272448-H-Hx)
- Inline validation: "Ideally, all validation should be inline—as soon as the user finishes filling in a field, an indicator should appear nearby if there's an error" — [NN/g, 10 Design Guidelines for Reporting Errors in Forms](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/errors-forms-design-guidelines/) (snippet only)
- Baymard's inline-validation testing is at [Baymard, Usability Testing of Inline Form Validation](https://baymard.com/blog/inline-form-validation) (blocked; not verified this session); its "8 form fields" finding concerns checkout, not expert forms — [Baymard via snippet](https://baymard.com/blog/inline-form-validation)
- Wizards "are recommended for situations where users are unfamiliar with the process — either because they lack domain expertise or because they rarely go through that process"; "if you expect some users will perform it repeatedly, consider offering them a faster alternative"; Alan Cooper: wizards "segregate new and expert users, with experts rarely using them and beginners soon desiring to discard them" — [NN/g, Wizards: Definition and Design Recommendations](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/wizards/) (snippet only)
- NN/g's four principles for reducing cognitive load in forms — [NN/g, Few Guesses, More Success](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/4-principles-reduce-cognitive-load/) (title only)
- GOV.UK's review page and task-list patterns (see Q3) are the best-documented public patterns for long, multi-section, multi-actor transactions — [GOV.UK Check answers](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-design-system/blob/main/src/patterns/check-answers/index.md); [GOV.UK Task list](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/task-list/)
- Carbon's data-table density ladder (24–64 px rows) and Salt's density tokens give concrete row-height references (see Q1).
- Fitts's law: time to acquire a target grows with distance and shrinks with target size; in dense grids "icons in a tight grid shrink W and raise D between neighbors, so the same screen real estate buys slower, fussier aiming" — [Laws of UX, Fitts's Law](https://lawsofux.com/fittss-law/) (blocked); [jacob.blog notes on Fitts's law](https://jacob.blog/notes/fitts-law/) (snippet only). Hick's law: decision time grows with the number of choices — [Hick's law, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hick%27s_law) (blocked; general statement)

### Inferences
- Schema-driven decision forms should render as a single scrollable surface with a sticky progress/section rail (task-list style) rather than a modal wizard; the "walk-through" is a linear sequence of sections, each independently completable, ending in a check-answers page. This satisfies both NN/g's wizard guidance and GOV.UK's multi-actor pattern.
- Validation policy: validate on field completion (blur), never on keystroke for numeric fields; show positive confirmation for mandate-critical fields; surface policy-gate results as soon as the inputs they depend on are complete, so hard-gate hits appear before the PM reaches the exception step.
- Because expert users plateau, gnosis should invest in visible accelerators (shortcut hints in menus, "press ? for shortcuts", recently used decision types in the palette) rather than assuming discovery.

### Gaps
- The eight NN/g complex-application guidelines could not be listed verbatim (page blocked; no mirror lists all eight).
- No quantitative evidence on wizard abandonment for expert/B2B users was found; consumer form-conversion statistics (e.g., IvyForms) were blocked and are not reliable for this context.
- Baymard's inline-validation findings (on-blur vs live, premature validation) were not verifiable this session.

## Key Question 7: Marketing-site benchmarks for B2B fintech and the performance cost of Three.js hero scenes

### Takeaway
No competitor marketing site (Aladdin, Enfusion, Bipsync, Alpha Theory, Essentia) could be inspected in this session. What is verifiable is the engineering guidance for a Three.js hero: render on demand, cap draw calls and pixel ratio, lazy-load the canvas behind real HTML, and honour `prefers-reduced-motion` with a static fallback; the page must still meet LCP ≤ 2.5 s and CLS ≤ 0.1 at p75.

### Cited Findings
- React Three Fiber performance guidance: set `frameloop="demand"` to render only when needed; scale resolution by multiplying `devicePixelRatio` by a 0–1 performance factor and call `regress()` during interaction to temporarily lower quality; keep draw calls "under 1,000 maximum, ideally a few hundred" using `InstancedMesh`; use LOD (`<Detailed>`) and cache geometries/materials via `useLoader`; use `PerformanceMonitor` to adjust quality against FPS thresholds — [react-three-fiber, Scaling performance (GitHub docs)](https://github.com/pmndrs/react-three-fiber/blob/master/docs/advanced/scaling-performance.mdx)
- Lazy-loaded hero pattern: semantic HTML loads eagerly, the Three.js canvas loads lazily; do not load the animation on small screens or cellular connections; respect `saveData`; tree-shake Three.js via ES modules — [Gatsby, Performance Optimization for three.js Web Animations](https://www.gatsbyjs.com/blog/performance-optimization-for-three-js-web-animations/) (snippet only; blocked); demo repo [PaulieScanlon/lazy-load-hero](https://github.com/PaulieScanlon/lazy-load-hero)
- MDN: use `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)` to "tone down the animation to avoid vestibular motion triggers"; "certain kinds of motion can be a trigger for Vestibular disorders, epilepsy, and migraine"; provide a mechanism to pause or disable animation — [MDN prefers-reduced-motion (GitHub source)](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/css/reference/at-rules/@media/prefers-reduced-motion/index.md); [MDN Web Animations API accessibility note](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/api/web_animations_api/index.md)
- A widely repeated claim that "approximately 27% of users may rely on reduced motion settings" appears in a secondary blog without a primary citation — [Bryan Whiting, Three.js hero animation](https://www.bryanwhiting.com/ai/building-a-high-performance-threejs-hero-animation/) (snippet only; unverified — do not quote as fact)
- "100 Three.js tips" (2026) covers loading strategy, pausing the render loop off-screen, pixel-ratio caps and bundle size — [Utsubo, Three.js best practices](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/threejs-best-practices-100-tips) (title only; blocked)
- Core Web Vitals thresholds apply to the marketing site: LCP ≤ 2.5 s, INP ≤ 200 ms, CLS ≤ 0.1 at p75 — [web.dev LCP](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/web.dev/blob/main/src/site/content/en/metrics/lcp/index.md), [INP](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/web.dev/blob/main/src/site/content/en/metrics/inp/index.md), [CLS](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/web.dev/blob/main/src/site/content/en/metrics/cls/index.md)
- Salt's low density is "Best for data-light or content-focused interfaces, such as marketing and communications, where a spacious layout enhances readability" — [Salt density](https://github.com/jpmorganchase/salt-ds/blob/main/site/docs/foundations/density.mdx)

### Inferences
- The hero should ship a static poster (image or SVG) as the LCP candidate, with the WebGL canvas mounted after LCP via dynamic import and IntersectionObserver; users with `prefers-reduced-motion`, `saveData`, narrow viewports or low `deviceMemory` get the poster only. Cap `dpr` at ~1.5–2, use `frameloop="demand"` or pause when off-screen/tab hidden, and budget draw calls in the low hundreds.
- Reserve the canvas a fixed aspect-ratio box so mounting it causes no layout shift (CLS ≤ 0.1).
- Because the marketing site is the only "generous whitespace" surface, use Salt-style low density there and keep the app at medium/high density; the contrast reinforces the app's clinical register.

## Key Question 8: What makes enterprise fintech UI feel "clinical" and trustworthy (typography, whitespace, colour restraint, consistency, latency) and what critics/design systems say

### Takeaway
Across the sources that were reachable, "trustworthy" is operationalised as: unchanging tokens across modes and densities (consistency), colour used only for meaning and always paired with a second cue (restraint), content over chrome with navigation moved to the keyboard (density with hierarchy), sub-second feedback (latency), explicit labelling of AI provenance and human overrides (honesty), and visible time semantics for records (auditability). Published critic reviews of specific 2023–2026 fintech UIs were not retrievable; the strongest evidence is design-system documentation.

### Cited Findings
- Consistency across states: Salt keeps "all components and tokens... identical across modes—only colors change" and requires that "switching density must not change the experience beyond presentation (e.g., spacing, text size)" — [Salt modes](https://github.com/jpmorganchase/salt-ds/blob/main/site/docs/foundations/modes.mdx); [Salt density](https://github.com/jpmorganchase/salt-ds/blob/main/site/docs/foundations/density.mdx)
- Colour restraint and redundancy: Carbon requires two of colour/shape/symbol and 3:1 contrast for status — [Carbon status indicators](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/main/src/pages/patterns/status-indicator-pattern/index.mdx); Carbon for AI limits its gradient "light spread... for readability" — [Carbon for AI](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/main/src/pages/guidelines/carbon-for-ai/index.mdx)
- Typographic order: Salt fixes line height at 1.3× and separates Label/Notation/Code styles from Body — [Salt typography](https://github.com/jpmorganchase/salt-ds/blob/main/site/docs/foundations/typography.mdx); tabular lining figures are the conventional treatment for columns of money — [Fonts.com](https://www.myfonts.com/pages/fontscom-learning-fontology-level-3-numbers-proportional-vs-tabular-figures); [MDN](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/css/reference/properties/font-variant-numeric/index.md)
- Density with hierarchy: Bloomberg's own UX group notes the difficulty of "clarifying the information hierarchy" on a "tight, information-dense screen" — [Bloomberg UX](https://www.bloomberg.com/company/what-we-do/ux/) (snippet only); the "content, not navigation" reading — [KlindrOS](https://klindros.com/blog/inside-the-twilight-trading-floor-the-design-system-behind-klindros)
- Latency as trust: Nielsen's 0.1 s / 1 s limits — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/) (mirror-verified); Linear's local-first sync engine as the reference for perceived speed — [performance.dev](https://performance.dev/how-is-linear-so-fast-a-technical-breakdown) (snippet only)
- Honesty about AI: label AI content, strip the label on human edit, keep a revert — [Carbon for AI](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/main/src/pages/guidelines/carbon-for-ai/index.mdx); say what the system cannot do and how well it performs — [HAX G1/G2](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/haxtoolkit/ai-guidelines/); operate only within knowledge limits — [NISTIR 8312](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.IR.8312)
- Auditability as trust: two clocks and immutable system time — [XTDB Time in XTDB](https://github.com/xtdb/xtdb/blob/main/docs/src/content/docs/about/time-in-xtdb.md); frozen pending payloads and full action logs — [Opcito](https://www.opcito.com/blogs/maker-checker-implementation-guide-for-secure-fintech-systems), [XTRM](https://blog.xtrm.com/posts/maker-checker-process) (snippets only)
- Informed choice as trust: Salt's "Guiding conscious decisions" pattern for accessibility/compliance/governance-affecting options — [Salt experience customization](https://github.com/jpmorganchase/salt-ds/blob/main/site/docs/patterns/experience-customization.mdx)

### Inferences
- A "clinical" gnosis style guide can be stated as eight testable rules, each traceable above: (1) one neutral type family with lining tabular figures for all numbers; (2) a five-state semantic colour scale used only for state, each state with an icon and label, 3:1 apart; (3) medium density default, high density opt-in with notice, tokens otherwise unchanged; (4) dark and light modes that change colour tokens only, dark recommended for all-day use; (5) command palette plus mnemonic shortcuts so the screen shows data not navigation; (6) ≤ 100 ms feedback and ≤ 1 s task latency with skeletons beyond; (7) AI label on every generated element until a human edits/approves it, plus a visible tool trace; (8) every record shows its two clocks and seal status.
- Where evidence is thin (critic reviews, competitor screens), the report should present these as design-system-derived requirements rather than as "what the market leaders do".

- No 2023–2026 design-critic articles or usability studies reviewing specific institutional fintech UIs (Aladdin, Charles River, Enfusion, Koyfin) were retrievable; Mobbin/Dribbble were deliberately not used.
- Goldman Sachs Design System principles and Morningstar's typography/colour specifics could not be read (blocked or removed from public access).
- Vercel, Stripe and Linear design documentation was not reachable; their inclusion above rests on secondary descriptions only.
